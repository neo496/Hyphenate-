How do you price software that replaces work instead of tooling?

My answer: charge per unit of finished work (a reconciled item, a closed account), with a floor and a ceiling. Seats punish the vendor for succeeding, and a percentage of money moved charges for dollars when the cost of the work is items. Finance buyers will accept this only if they can predict the bill and audit what they were charged for.

The Fin case is useful because it shows both sides. Reviewers praise paying only for results, but G2 reviews flag bill unpredictability, and critics object to what counts as a “resolution.” Lesson for finance: an outcome-based price lives or dies on a definition of “outcome” the controller agrees with before signing.

Test it: three pricing models, one customer

Where each model breaks

  • Per seat. The better the agent works, the fewer people the customer needs, so vendor revenue falls as customer value rises. It also prices tools for humans, not work done.
  • Percent of volume (the 0.5% idea). A $2M wire and a $200 invoice take the same effort to reconcile but pay the vendor 10,000 times apart. Try the average-item-size slider: the fee swings wildly while the work doesn’t. It also puts the vendor near money movement, which raises regulatory and trust questions a controller will ask first.
  • Per finished item. Cost tracks work, but bills spike with volume. Fix with a monthly cap and a minimum, and with a plain definition of “finished” that the audit log can prove.

What I’d test next

  • Interview 5–10 controllers: would you accept a per-item price with a monthly cap? What do you need to see to believe an item is “done”?
  • Replace the placeholder defaults above with real volumes and minutes from a pilot customer.
  • Check whether exceptions humans must resolve should be free, discounted, or billed.
